Savla Kul Utpatti

How the Savla nukh came to be: from the Chavda Rajputs of Anhilpur Patan, through the Jain dharma taken in Samvat 1255, to the Kutchi Visa Oswal households that worship Shri Siriyal Mataji at Bhinsara today.

In one breath

Who we are

Savla is one of the four Chavda nukhs of the Kutchi Visa Oswal community, alongside Dedhia, Chheda and Nandu. Our ancestor Devaḍaji Chavda of Anhilpur Patan accepted the Jain dharma from Acharya Jayasimhasuri of the Anchalgachha on Bhadarva Sud 5, Vikram Samvat 1255 (1198 CE). His son Jetsinh founded the Savla nukh. The family moved through Marwad and Sindh and settled in Kutch under the Jadeja rulers of Kanthkot in the 14th–15th century. Our Kuldevi is Shri Sarayal Mataji, known today as Siriyal Maa, whose devasthan is at Bhinsara.

Samvat 1255

The conversion at Patan

In the reign of the Solankis, the Chavda houses that had once ruled Gujarat still held land and standing around Anhilpur Patan. Among them was Devaḍaji Chavda, son of Umeraj. On the fifth day of the bright half of Bhadarva, in the year 1255 of the Vikram era, he heard the twelve vratas expounded by Acharya Jayasimhasuri, the second pattadhar of the Anchalgachha, and with his household accepted the Jain dharma. The family entered the Oswal jnati.

Devaḍaji had four sons. From each a nukh descends, and each nukh took its own Kuldevi. From Jetsinh, the Savlas.

Samvat 1255 in every source. The year is carried by our family record (the Barot notebook), by the Chheda vanshvruksh, by the Anchalgachha Mahoti Pattavali, and by the Anchalgachha Digdarshan (¶346: "જેસલમેરમાં સં. ૧૨૫૫માં દેવડ નામના ચાવડા રજપૂતને પ્રતિબોધ આપીને જયસિંહસૂરિએ જૈનધર્મી કર્યો અને ઓશવાળ જ્ઞાતિમાં ભેળવ્યો"). The only variance is the place: the family record and the poster say Anhilpur Patan; the gachha sources say Jaisalmer. The Mandal follows the family record for the place and lists the Jaisalmer reading on the alternate accounts page.
The founding line

From Vanraj to the four nukhs

1
Vanraj Chavda
Founder of Anhilpur Patan, 8th century. The Barot chopdi begins our line here. Tradition rather than documented descent.
·
Chavda houses of Patan and Kutch
Chavda rule of Gujarat ended in 942 CE, but Chavda branches continued as chiefs, including at Patgadh and Vagham in western Kutch until the 13th century.
2
Devaḍaji Chavda, son of Umeraj
Took the twelve vratas from Jayasimhasuri at Anhilpur Patan, Bhadarva Sud 5, VS 1255. Entered the Oswal jnati.
3
Jetsinh
Second son of Devaḍaji. Founder of the Savla nukh. The Digdarshan (¶347–348) names another son, Āmar, who built a shikharbaddha Adinath temple at Jalore for 1,70,000 tanka, and whose own son Dedhia gave the Dedhia gotra its name.

The Savla line continues
The generation-by-generation chopdi from Jetsinh onward is held by the Jamnagar Bhatt Raja family and is being obtained by the Mandal. The Chheda poster runs fifteen generations; the Savla list should run the same.
Marwad · Sindh · Kutch

How we reached Kutch

Anhilpur Patan
Seat of the Chavda line and place of the conversion. Kamleshwar Mahadev on the Sarasvati remains the kuldev. The chopdi traces the family further back to Ayodhya.
Osian, Marwad
The Oswal homeland. After joining the Oswal jnati the family is recorded among the mahajans of Osian.
Thar Parkar, Sindh
Under Muslim rule. The mahajans were told that to stay they must accept roti-beti and the kalma; the Badshah set a guard on them. Two brothers, the Bhat and the Gorji, went to Satmaram Kumar Sumro, a Hindu prince of the Sumras, sang his birdavali and won a boon: the Oswals' release. The Bhat went back for the mahajans, saw the guards still in place, and told the Guruji, "I have done my duty; now do yours." By the power of his vow the Guruji left the guards blind and deaf, and the mahajans left with their households on bullock carts. Their names are still written first at the start of any auspicious work.
Kanthkot, Kutch
Old name Garkapuri. Received by the family of Jam Dedaji, the Kutch naresh and the Rayal Jam of Bara, c. VS 1300–1454. The mahajans were divided among Kandi, Sakhda-sama and the Garh. First Vagad village: Lakadia.
Halar
A section went with Jam Rawal to Jamnagar (1540) and settled at Kansumra; these became the Halai Oswals.

The Barot record counts 170 villages in four divisions: 52 in Kutch (Kutchi), 24 in Vagad (Vagad Oswal), 42 in Sakhda, and the Halar settlements (Halai). Jam Dedaji of Kanthkot and Jam Rawal of Nawanagar are historical rulers, so the frame holds even where individual details are tradition.

ઉચાળા · The leaving of Sindh

"Ke Osvale uchala"

The community kept the reason for the migration in verse. This Kutchi duha, sung in the kul, dates the leaving to Samvat 1350 (c. 1293 CE), the years in which the Khilji sultans took Sindh and Gujarat, and names what drove the Oswals out. Each verse ends with the same line: ke Osvale uchala, "and so the Oswals packed up and left."

In thirteen hundred and fifty, in Sindh, the troubles grew heavy;
for dharma, for honour, for shame — and so the Oswals left.

Under Khilji's rule the governors raged;
under the Sayyids' terror — and so the Oswals left.

Temples broken, derasars broken, locks broken on the treasuries;
raids by day and by night — and so the Oswals left.

No Rao would listen; the killers had their way;
no woman was safe — and so the Oswals left.

Some to the derasar, some wandering, a yati here, a bava with his beads;
the blood of the cattle flowed — and so the Oswals left.

No justice, no judge, no keeper of the law;
in the terror of the Muslim rule — and so the Oswals left.

The people were crushed, the burdens cruel;
convert or die — and so the Oswals left.

Goods looted, homes looted, children carried off by the Arabs;
sold as slaves to the Arabs — and so the Oswals left.

Bundles on their heads they fled; for the leaving there was no shore;
bare heads, bare feet, blisters on the feet.
From the terror of the Sayyids, from the terror of the Arabs,
the Oswals left, the Oswals left.

On the date. Samvat 1350 is about a century after Devaḍaji's conversion (1255) and sits between the Osian years and the arrival at Kanthkot (c. VS 1300–1454 in the Barot record). Alauddin Khilji's armies took Gujarat in 1299 and Sindh soon after; the Sumra dynasty of Thar Parkar was under pressure in the same years. The poem and the notebook describe the same moment from two sides: the notebook tells how the escape was managed, the poem why it was necessary.
